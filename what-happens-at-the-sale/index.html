Before a foreclosure or tax sale

If a sale is coming up, it may bring in more than is owed. That extra money could be yours.

Most people heading into a foreclosure or tax sale are never told what happens to that leftover amount.

What leftover funds can mean, even before the sale happens

What happens on the day of a foreclosure or tax sale?

The property is offered at auction under the rules in your notice. That notice is the best source for the time, the place, and the type of sale. A mortgage foreclosure and a tax sale are not the same process, but in both cases the winning bid is applied to allowed costs and to the debt being collected.

This page is general. It cannot replace the notice you received, or advice from a lawyer in your state.

What if the sale brings in more than is owed?

If the winning bid is higher than the debt being collected, plus the allowed costs, the difference may be leftover funds. Other valid claims may still be paid before any remainder would go to the owner. Many sales do not produce leftover funds. We cannot promise that yours will.

Does this work the same way for a tax lien, a tax deed, or a mortgage foreclosure?

No, and that is worth knowing before the sale date.

A mortgage foreclosure sale, if it brings in more than the mortgage and costs, can create surplus funds.

A tax deed sale or a tax resale, if it brings in more than the taxes and costs, can create excess proceeds. In some states the former owner also has a redemption period after a tax sale, which is a different right from a leftover-funds claim.

A tax lien or tax-certificate sale is often only a sale of the tax debt. Leftover funds, if they ever exist, may not appear until a later deed sale or foreclosure of that lien.

If you are not sure which notice you have, bring it to the free consultation and we will help you identify it.

Will I get money if the property is sold at auction?

Only if leftover funds actually exist after costs and valid claims, and only if a claim is made the way your state requires. The money is often held by a trustee, a sheriff, a county official, or a court. It may not be mailed to you on its own, especially if they only have the property address.

What can I do now to protect a future claim, without signing away my rights?

Keep every notice. Write down the sale date, the county, and the name of the trustee, sheriff, or tax office.

Give that office a current mailing address, in writing if you can, so any later notice about leftover funds can reach you.

After the sale, ask whether there were excess proceeds and what form or petition they use.

Do not ignore mail that looks official, and do not assume a private letter is official.

You should be careful about signing a contract before the sale. In some states, an agreement signed at the wrong time can be limited or void. Do not sign anything you do not understand. You can talk with us, or with a licensed attorney in that state, first.

Can you stop the sale, or should I still talk to you?

We cannot stop the sale, and we will not tell you that we can. If you want to explore keeping the property, contact your lender or the tax office, and consider a HUD-approved housing counselor, as soon as you can. You can find HUD counseling information at https://www.hud.gov/helping-americans/housing-counseling.

Still curious about what comes next? A few more answers are below

What is the difference between surplus, excess proceeds, and excess funds?

They are different names for leftover money after a sale. "Surplus" is common after a mortgage foreclosure. "Excess funds" or "excess proceeds" is common after a tax sale. An "overage" is the same idea.

What if nobody bids, or the bid is short?

Then there may be no leftover funds. In some mortgage cases, a shortfall can raise other issues, such as a deficiency. That is outside the leftover-funds help we describe on this page, and we will not pretend otherwise.

What if I have a right to redeem after a tax sale?

Some states give the former owner a period to redeem, meaning to pay the required amount and recover the property. That is a different path from claiming leftover funds. Deadlines are often short. Talk to a lawyer in that state, or to the tax office on your notice, before you rely on redemption. We do not sell redemption services on this page.

Should I sign a recovery agreement before the sale?

Not without understanding it, and not if your state limits or voids certain pre-sale or same-day agreements. A free consultation does not require you to sign. If we ever offer a written agreement, you will have time to read it and to ask questions.

I got a letter about leftover money, and the sale has not happened yet. Is it real?

It might be a real company writing early, or it might be a letter that overstates what anyone can know before the auction. No one can honestly promise an amount before the sale is complete and the costs and claims are tallied. Check the sender, and do not pay anyone up front to "reserve" funds.
