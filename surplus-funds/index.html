Could leftover money from the sale still belong to you?

What is leftover money from a foreclosure or a tax sale?

When a property is sold at a mortgage foreclosure, or at a tax sale or tax-deed auction, the winning bid is used to pay the sale costs and the debt being collected. If the bid is higher than that amount, the difference is leftover money.

In a mortgage foreclosure, people usually call this surplus funds or excess proceeds. In a tax sale, they often say excess funds, excess proceeds, or an overage.

A tax lien sale is different from a tax deed sale. In a tax lien or tax-certificate sale, the buyer is usually buying the tax debt, not the property, so leftover sale funds often do not appear at that moment. Leftover funds are more likely later, if the property itself is sold or the lien is foreclosed and the sale brings in more than is owed.

Do I get the extra money if the sale brought in more than was owed?

You might, but not automatically, and not in every case.

Valid sale costs are paid first. Then the debt that was being collected. Then other people with a valid claim, such as a later mortgage, a judgment, or an HOA lien, may be paid in order. If anything remains, the former owner, or the owner's heirs, may have a claim to it.

Some sales do not produce any leftover funds at all. We will not tell you that money exists until the record has been checked, and we cannot promise a recovery.

How do I find out if a county, trustee, or court is holding funds?

The holder depends on the type of sale and the state.

After some mortgage foreclosures, the leftover money is held by a trustee, a sheriff, or a court. After many tax sales, it is held by a county treasurer, a tax commissioner, a clerk, or a court. If it sits unclaimed long enough, it may be sent to the state's unclaimed-property office.

The notice, if one was sent, often goes to the property address. If nobody lives there anymore, it is easy to miss.

You can usually call the office that handled the sale and ask whether excess proceeds were held. You are not required to use a private company to ask that question. If you would like us to look at the public record with you, that review is free.

How do I claim the funds, and can I do it myself?

In many places, yes, you can file on your own. The office holding the money will often have a form, or the claim is made by a petition in court. Each state, and sometimes each county, has its own steps and deadlines.

Some people are comfortable doing that, especially when the claim is simple and the office is easy to reach. Other people want help, especially when there are heirs, competing claims, or a court filing.

If you want our help, we will explain what we would do, and what our fee would be, in writing, before you sign anything. You can still choose to file on your own.

Is this legitimate? I got a letter, or I found this online.

It is fair to ask. Leftover funds after a foreclosure or a tax sale are real, and they are a matter of public record. The process is also a place where some companies overpromise, hide a fee, or pretend to be the government.

We will not ask you for money up front to check the record. We will not ask for your Social Security number just to see whether funds exist. We will encourage you to confirm anything we tell you with the county, the trustee, or the court yourself.

If you received a letter, check whether it names the county and the sale. Then call the office using a phone number you find on the office's own website, not only the number in the letter.

How long do I have, and how long does a claim take?

Both answers depend on the state and on the type of sale. A tax-sale deadline is often different from a mortgage-foreclosure deadline. Some clocks start at the sale, some start when a notice is mailed, and some change once the money is sent to unclaimed property.

In general, it is better not to wait, but a message that says "you will lose this money tomorrow" is a reason to slow down and check.

Once a claim is filed, some are resolved in a few months, and some take longer, especially if there are heirs or competing claims. We will not promise a date.

What is the difference between a tax lien and a tax deed?

In a tax lien or tax-certificate sale, the county is usually selling the unpaid tax debt. The buyer earns interest or a penalty if the owner pays. The property itself often has not been sold yet, so there may be no leftover sale funds at that stage.

In a tax deed sale, the property is auctioned. In a redeemable-deed sale, the property is sold but the former owner may still have a period to buy it back. Leftover funds, if any, are more likely at the deed or resale stage than at a certificate sale.

Utah, Idaho, Georgia, and Oklahoma each have their own tax-sale systems. Georgia, for example, uses a redeemable tax deed. A leftover-funds claim is not the same thing as redeeming the property.

If the county sold a tax lien, not the house, is there money for me right now?

Often, not yet. Leftover funds usually appear only if the property is later sold for more than the tax debt and costs. If you are not sure which kind of sale happened, tell us what you know, or send a copy of the notice, and we will help you sort out the type of sale during the free consultation.

Can the county keep the extra money from a tax foreclosure?

In 2023, the U.S. Supreme Court held in Tyler v. Hennepin County that a government generally may not keep the value above the tax debt when it takes and sells a home for unpaid taxes. States still differ on how you claim that leftover amount, who is paid first, and how long you have. The county or court process still has to be followed.

Who is paid before the former owner?

Sale costs and the debt being collected come first. Other recorded liens and claims may come next, in order of priority. This is one reason a sale can bring in more than the first mortgage, or more than the taxes, and still leave little or nothing for the former owner.

What if the owner has died?

Heirs or the estate may still have a claim, but the office or court will usually need proof of authority, such as probate or heirship documents. We can talk through what we see and, where a lawyer is needed, work with licensed attorney partners. This is not a substitute for estate advice.

Do I need a lawyer or a company?

Not always. Many claims can be filed by the former owner directly with the county, trustee, or court. A lawyer is more often useful when the claim is contested, when the owner has died, or when the state or county requires a court petition. North Peak Recovery is not a law firm. Where a filing must be made by an attorney, we work with licensed attorneys in that state.

What if I still owe money, or I think there was a deficiency?

If the sale brought in less than was owed, there may be no leftover funds, and in some situations a deficiency can still be an issue. That is a different question from a surplus or excess-funds claim. We will be honest if the record does not show leftover money.
